Whereabouts is a lightweight software platform for tourism destinations, currently used by about 30 organizations and 10,000 users across Canada and the US. It replaces the aging systems most destination marketing organizations run on and is made up of various modules to manage everything from business listings and contacts to events and travel itineraries. Besides co-founding the company, I've served as its sole designer since 2022.

Nearly every destination management organization (or DMO, for short) tries to maintain its own private list of tourism businesses in its region, using everything from massively overcomplicated CRMs to scattered spreadsheets. Slowly but surely, the data drifts, leaving the DMO without a reliable, single source of truth. Visitors are the ones who find out: a phone number that's been disconnected, summer hours showing in February, a supposedly open motel that's been closed for years.

Interviews I conducted with our clients also revealed a very different reality of tourism marketing than I had assumed: administrative borders aren't as strict as they might seem, and DMOs routinely need to share business listings with neighbouring or enclosing destinations. This key insight made clear that the obvious build — driven by per-org data silos that would have eventually gone stale — would be a non-starter for many prospects, thus limiting our potential reach.

These findings tilted my earliest decisions toward a cross-org, wiki-style data model; instead of a DMO being required to create and steward possibly hundreds if not thousands of listings, each business would get a shareable record (managed by the business's staff directly) in our global Whereabouts database, to which any DMO could subscribe and include in their Directory.

The discussions that I held with our engineering team highlighted some important costs that would come with such a model: namely, more complex permissions and possible confusion around data governance, both of which could weigh down an otherwise simple and intuitive product experience. Despite this, and given the clear benefits it would provide clients, I successfully argued for the shared model knowing its price.

The shared data model I specced out and advocated for also created a clearer distinction between data management and publishing. The source of truth, a single business record, would remain narrowly editable (DMO admins shouldn't be changing evergreen info provided by a business, like phone numbers, URLs, or hours of operation), while overrides like custom descriptions and featured images would live in the publishing workflow, where overriding is the expected action.

I prototyped the module end to end in Figma, from wireframes through high-fidelity screens, working through the architecture with our CTO and two developers. As a result, nearly half of our clients migrated from entrenched but bloated and costly CRMs like Salesforce and SimpleView.
